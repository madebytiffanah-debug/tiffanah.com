Six Streams, Then One Line Below Them

1
Six department P&Ls
F&B, Talent, VIP/Bottle Service, Merch, Ticketing, and Sponsorships each get their own revenue, cost, and profit line — exactly like the single-department statements from Course 2.
2
Combined departmental profit
Add all six together and you get combined departmental profit — the number that looks like “how the night did,” but isn't the final answer yet.
3
Shared overhead comes last
Security, cleaning, and utilities aren't charged to any one department — they're subtracted from the combined total, and they're the line most likely to turn a good-looking night into a loss.

MODULE 2

The Talent Settlement

Tool: Artist Settlement Calculator

8

Guarantee vs. Door Deal

Two ways to structure what an artist gets paid

GUARANTEE
  • A fixed fee, regardless of attendance
  • Artist's risk is low; the venue carries the downside if the show underperforms
  • Best for: predictable costs, a known or proven draw
DOOR DEAL / SPLIT
  • Artist pay tied to a percentage of ticket revenue
  • Both sides share the upside and downside of attendance
  • Best for: uncertain draw, aligning incentives with the venue

Many real deals blend both — a base guarantee plus a split once ticket revenue clears an agreed threshold.

9

How a Settlement Actually Closes

1
Two budgets set at booking
Hospitality and tech each get their own agreed budget when the deal is signed — well before anyone knows the actual show-night numbers.
2
Actual costs tracked separately through the show
Real hospitality spend and real tech/production spend are each tracked as the show comes together, and each is compared against its own original budget.
3
Each overage backed out at settlement
If either actual spend exceeds its own budget, that difference is backed out against what's owed to the artist when the show closes — not quietly absorbed, and not renegotiated after the fact.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Loss Leaders, and the Line Below Everything

1
What makes something a loss leader
A stream priced to break even, or even lose a little, specifically because it drives spend somewhere else — cheap bar pricing that keeps people in the building buying tickets and merch.
2
Loss leaders need consolidated thinking
Judged alone, a loss-leader department looks like it's failing. Judged by its actual job — driving revenue elsewhere — it may be working exactly as intended.
3
Overhead is the real scoreboard
Whatever the department mix, security, cleaning, and utilities still come off the top of the combined total — and that line, not any single department's P&L, decides whether the night actually made money.
14

KEY TAKEAWAY

A loss leader isn't a mistake to fix. It's a strategy to measure correctly — by its effect on the whole show, not its own line.
